## Q4-a: Bias amplification in the g-computation algorithm for time-varying treatments: a case study of industry payments and prescription of opioid products

* **Citation:** BMC Medical Research Methodology (2022) 22:120; DOI 10.1186/s12874-022-01563-3; PMID 35468735; PMCID PMC9036763
* **Access:** Open access, CC BY 4.0 (licence statement on PDF p. 1 and in the PMC XML `<license>`). Retrieved: the publisher PDF, the PMC XML and Additional file 1 (publisher ESM store).

### Setting

Two binary time points (T1, T2), a binary time-varying confounder X2 affected by T1, and a binary outcome Y, all generated by logistic models. The unmeasured confounder X1 is a binary common cause of T1, T2 and Y. The instrument X3 is a time-fixed common cause of T1 and T2:
  * scenario A: nine standard-normal perfect instruments, with odds ratios 2, 5 or 10 on T1 and on T2;
  * scenario B: one near-instrument X3B ~ N(β1·X1, 1) with OR 10 on both treatments and β1 from 0.01 to 0.3.

The estimator is parametric g-computation with logistic models that omit X1. The estimands are the static regime contrasts E[Y^(t1,t2)] − E[Y^(0,0)] for (1,0), (0,1) and (1,1). There were 10,000 datasets per N (500; 10,000; 200,000). Truth was computed from N = 10,000,000. A physician-payment case study used the same algorithm with linear models.

> "conditioning on variables that are associated with the outcome only through the treatment, called instrumental variables (IVs), can increase the bias of treatment effect estimates in the point-treatment settings under the presence of unmeasured confounding" (p. 2, Background)

> "However, it has been still unclear whether and the extent to which this bias amplifying feature of IVs and near-IVs can be applied to the time-varying treatment settings." (p. 2, Background)

> "there can be IV or near-IV for each time-point which may influence the g-computation estimator in an unpredictable way through distinct regression models in the presence of unmeasured (time-fixed or time-varying) confounders" (p. 2, Background)

> "we aimed to evaluate the difference in biases due to unmeasured confounding between models with and without adjustment of perfect IV (scenario A) or near-IV (scenario B) of the time-varying treatments" (p. 4, Methods, Monte Carlo simulation study)

> "In both scenarios, we simulated 10,000 datasets of sample size (N) = 500, 10,000, and 200,000, respectively." (p. 4, Methods, Monte Carlo simulation study)

> "X1 was drawn from independent Bernoulli distributions with parameter 0.5." (p. 4, Methods, scenario A)

> "Common cause of treatment at time 1, treatment at time 2, and outcome" (p. 6, Fig. 2 caption)

> "Common cause of treatment at time 1 and treatment at time 2" (p. 6, Fig. 2 caption)

> "We first fit two logistic regression models" (p. 5, Methods, statistical analysis)

> "True values of marginal expectations were approximately obtained in large (N = 10,000,000) sample" (p. 5, Methods, statistical analysis)

### What is shown

* **Scenario A (perfect instruments), N = 200,000** (Supplementary Table 2):
  * Bias without the instrument: 5.63, 4.33 and 4.27 percentage points for (1,0), (0,1) and (1,1).
  * The largest bias with an instrument (Model 10) is 5.92, 4.58 and 4.56, i.e. +0.29, +0.25 and +0.29 points (about +5 to +7 % relative). Standard errors are unchanged.
  * The increase is not uniform: Model 8 gives 5.61 for (1,0), slightly below 5.63.
* **Scenario B (near-instrument), N = 200,000** (Supplementary Table 3):
  * At β1 = 0.01, adjusting raises the bias from 1.55 / 4.32 / 2.14 to 1.92 / 4.66 / 2.37.
  * At β1 = 0.30, adjusting lowers it from 3.44 / 5.65 / 4.22 to 1.96 / 4.45 / 3.02.
* So the amplification shown is real but small in this data-generating process, and it reverses when the instrument is even weakly tied to the unmeasured confounder.
* **Two inconsistencies, both left unresolved by the paper:**
  * The main text gives the no-instrument biases as 5.5, 4.3 and −4.0 without naming N; the supplement gives 4.27 for (1,1) at N = 200,000.
  * Supplementary Table 2 is titled "Scenario B" although it lists the scenario-A instruments X3A_1–X3A_9, and the main text cites it for scenario A.

> "the biases due to the presence of unmeasured confounding were 5.5 for" (p. 6, Results, Monte Carlo)

> "The biases generally increased (i.e., away from the true value) when additionally adjusting for IV" (p. 6, Results, Monte Carlo)

> "These trends were less clear when the sample size was 500 compared to when the sample size was 10,000 or 200,000." (p. 6, Results, Monte Carlo)

> "We did not find a clear trend in standard error in the models adjusting for IV in the g-computation algorithm" (p. 6, Results, Monte Carlo)

> "Instead, the bias decreased after adjusting for near-IV when it had a stronger association with the unmeasured confounders" (p. 8, Discussion)

> "but the estimated effect was smaller than those in Model 1" (p. 6, Results, case study)

> "Supplementary Table 2. Scenario B: Bias of treatment effects in the model adjusting for IV (X3As) under the presence of unmeasured confounder (X1)" (Additional file 1, p. 3, Supplementary Table 2 (title))

> "Model 1 5.63 0.89 5.70 4.33 0.80 4.40 4.27 2.25 4.83" (Additional file 1, p. 3, Supplementary Table 2, N = 200,000, Model 1 (no IV): bias/SE/RMSE for (1,0), (0,1), (1,1)) [table row, layout text]

> "Model 10 5.92 0.89 5.98 4.58 0.80 4.65 4.56 2.23 5.08" (Additional file 1, p. 3, Supplementary Table 2, N = 200,000, Model 10 (+X3A_9)) [table row, layout text]

> "Model 8 5.61 0.88 5.68 4.55 0.80 4.62 4.38 2.24 4.92" (Additional file 1, p. 3, Supplementary Table 2, N = 200,000, Model 8 (+X3A_7)) [table row, layout text]

### Stated conditions and limitations

> "Our findings were not stable with a small sample size (N = 500)" (p. 8, Discussion)

> "we need subject matter knowledge to determine whether the variable is a perfect IV or near-IV" (p. 9, Discussion)

> "possible residual confounding would largely outweigh bias amplification due to including such a variable in the model, unless it has a very strong association with the exposure" (p. 9, Discussion)

> "Because our simulation results were obtained through a specific data-generating process based on a case study, future studies are needed to validate our findings in other time-varying settings (e.g., different parameter values, different scales, continuous treatments, etc.)." (p. 9, Discussion)

> "we found that adjusting for a perfect IV may amplify the bias even under the setting of time-varying treatments" (p. 9, Conclusions)

### How it differs from our study

* **Site/centre variable.** Their instrument is a generic standard-normal covariate (or one tied to X1 through β1). Ours is a categorical centre variable with 4 levels, which may also be related to unmeasured patient mix: the near-instrument regime in which their amplification reverses.
* **Modified treatment policy.** They contrast static regimes (always/never treat at each of two times); we target a sequential MTP, which shifts the observed treatment. Instrument adjustment for MTP estimands is not studied.
* **Sequential doubly robust estimator.** Parametric g-computation (logistic models without X1) versus our SDR estimator with flexible (MLP) or linear (X2) nuisance learners. Their result says nothing about how nuisance-learner flexibility interacts with an instrument.
* **Learned video representations.** Their instrument is either included or left out as an explicit covariate. In our FM and FM−C arms, site information enters implicitly through video embeddings and is only partly removed by LEACE, which is an intermediate case they do not study.
* **Window-selected sub-population.** No selection: full simulated samples. Our analysis is restricted to 158 window-selected cases.
* **Time points.** Two time points versus our multi-step windows.

## Q1-a: A framework for analyzing concept representations in neural models

* **Citation:** arXiv 2605.01381v1; CoNLL 2026, DOI 10.18653/v1/2026.conll-main.34 (the conference version was not retrieved; quotes are from arXiv v1)
* **Access:** Open access, arXiv, licence CC BY 4.0 (abstract page). Retrieved: arXiv PDF v1.

### Setting

* **Data:** frozen BERT text representations (gender, profession; pretrained and fine-tuned) and frozen HuBERT layer-11 speech frames (39 phone classes; 40 speakers).
* **Method:** a concept subspace is estimated by LEACE and four other estimators on a "space-train" split. Leakage is the accuracy of a linear probe trained on the erased complement, trained on a disjoint probe-train split and scored on a test split.
* For phones, the classes are shared across splits but the speakers are not.
* No time dimension and no causal estimation.

> "leakage as the accuracy when trained on its complement" (p. 3, §2.1 Containment and disentanglement)

> "In this paper, we illustrate the framework using linear probes." (p. 3, §2.1, footnote 3)

> "Prior work on concept erasure often uses the same sets for estimating concept subspaces and training probing classifiers (Ravfogel et al., 2022; Belrose et al., 2023), and under this setup, LEACE is guaranteed to have zero leakage." (p. 4, §3.2 Generalization of concept subspaces)

> "we use three disjoint sets to ensure that the evaluation reflects the generalization of both the concept subspaces and the classifiers" (p. 4, §3.2)

> "We focused on the 11th HuBERT layer" (p. 6, §5 Analyzing phone and speaker subspaces)

> "While the identical 39 phoneme classes were shared across the three data subsets, the speaker identities were disjoint." (p. 6, §5)

> "The space-train set had unique 40 speakers" (p. 6, §5)

### What is shown

* LEACE's zero-leakage guarantee holds only on the data it was fitted on.
  * On held-out HuBERT data, linear-probe leakage (accuracy, %) was 29.6 for phones (best case 7.8, worst 87.8) and 54.4 for speakers (best 2.7, worst 79.9). Source: Table 5, p. 13.
  * The same held-out leakage appeared for gender and profession in a fine-tuned BERT.
* The "overfit" setup, where the subspace is estimated on the probe's own data, gives optimal leakage.
* About the numbers: they are read from the layout text of p. 13. Table 6's "standard" row does not match Table 5 in that extraction, so it was not used.

> "LEACE, an estimator from the concept erasure literature (Belrose et al., 2023), finds concept subspaces with strong overall properties but exhibits leakage on unseen data, despite its no-leakage guarantee on seen data" (p. 2, §1 Introduction)

> "Despite LEACE’s good performance relative to others, we note that it still has substantial leakage (see the left plots), which shows that its no-leakage guarantee does not extend to unseen data." (p. 7, §5.1 Phone and speaker subspace estimation)

> "we found the same issue with generalization when evaluating gender and profession on a fine-tuned BERT model" (p. 7, §5.1)

> "These results indicate a wider problem with generalization in LEACE, and reveal important limitations in its use for concept erasure." (p. 7, §5.1)

> "The speaker subspaces estimated on space-train did not generalize well, indicating likely overfitting to the particular set of 40 speakers" (p. 7, §5.2 Speaker subspace generalization)

> "In particular, leakage is optimal, aligning with the proof shown by prior work (Belrose et al., 2023)." (p. 8, §5.2)

> "Concept Estimator Ret.↑ Leak.↓ Pur.↑ Int.↓" (p. 13, Appendix D, Table 5 (header)) [table row, layout text]

> "phones worst case 7.8 87.8 20.1 97.3" (p. 13, Appendix D, Table 5) [table row, layout text]

> "phones LEACE 82.6 29.6 94.3 21.2" (p. 13, Appendix D, Table 5) [table row, layout text]

> "phone best case 87.8 7.8 97.3 20.1" (p. 13, Appendix D, Table 5) [table row, layout text]

> "speakers worst case 2.7 79.9 12.2 92.2" (p. 13, Appendix D, Table 5) [table row, layout text]

> "speakers LEACE 48.9 54.4 88.3 12.5" (p. 13, Appendix D, Table 5) [table row, layout text]

> "speakers best case 79.9 2.7 92.2 12.2" (p. 13, Appendix D, Table 5) [table row, layout text]

### Stated conditions and limitations

> "Overall, our results suggest that estimating a robust concept space for speaker identity requires a large number of speakers as well as a large number of dimensions." (p. 8, §5.2)

> "even LEACE struggled in some cases to generalize beyond its training data" (p. 8, §6 Discussion and Conclusion)

> "Our evaluation assesses concept subspaces using only linear probes, while it is also possible that concept subspaces contains non-linear structures." (p. 11, Appendix A Limitations)

> "the conclusions about the specific concepts studied here may not hold for representations extracted from other models or datasets" (p. 11, Appendix A Limitations)

### How it differs from our study

* **Window-selected sub-population.** Their failure is on held-out frames or speakers, a different sample, not a selected subset of the fitting cases. In our step 5, the eraser held on the 278-case set (linear BA 0.250) and failed on its own 158-case window-selected analysis region. The closest analogue is their speaker concept: a source identity like our centre. But their test speakers are new identities, whereas our 4 centres are the same classes in both sets.
* **Learned video representations.** Text (BERT) and speech (HuBERT) representations, not surgical-video foundation-model embeddings after PCA.
* **Probe class.** Linear probes only (they list this as a limitation). Our nonlinear (MLP) site decodability is outside their evaluation.
* **Site/centre, MTP, SDR estimator.** No causal estimation, so none of these apply.

## Q1-b: Detecting a Shift Is Not Enough: Exact Minimax Limits of Linear Representation Repair

* **Citation:** arXiv 2610.08069v1 (6 Oct 2026; preprint)
* **Access:** Open access, arXiv, licence CC BY 4.0 (abstract page). Retrieved: arXiv PDF v1.

### Setting

* **Problem:** two sources (devices), a single mean-shift direction, and isotropic Gaussian contrasts.
* **Calibration:** paired recordings; one shared linear (or affine) editor under a hard distortion budget.
* **Metric:** the share of the population mean shift left on new data.
* **Theory:** exact minimax risk.
* **Empirics:**
  * simulations;
  * BOAS sleep EEG, wearable vs polysomnograph: 85 participants, d = 118, calibration pool 43 / test 42, 20 random splits;
  * a second EEG device pair.
* MP, SAL and binary LEACE share the same kernel. No causal estimation.

> "From such pairs we learn an editor: one linear map shared by both sources and held to a hard distortion budget, and on new data we measure the share of the source separation that remains." (p. 1, §1 Introduction)

> "Without pairs, the same problem is linear concept erasure." (p. 1, §1 Introduction)

> "We split participants at random into a calibration pool of 43 and a test set of 42" (p. 7, §7.2 A wearable versus a clinical recording)

> "fit the erasers, and measure on the test participants how much of the population mean shift remains, over 20 random splits" (p. 7, §7.2)

### What is shown

* A linear eraser fitted on finite calibration data leaves a predictable share of the source shift in new participants. In the EEG, that share is 0.69 with one calibration participant (128 epochs), about half with four, and 0.125 with 32.
* The closed-form prediction matches the observed residual: RMSD 0.016 over 80 cells.
* Detecting the shift is far easier than removing it.

> "Projecting out an inaccurately estimated direction removes ordinary variation while preserving much of the shift" (p. 1, §1 Introduction)

> "The three methods share the residual but not the distortion." (p. 5, §4 (exact cost of finite calibration))

> "the root-mean-square deviation over 80 cells is 0.016" (p. 7, §7.2)

> "for the actual MP, SAL and LEACE maps, whose kernels coincide" (p. 7, §7.2)

> "so even 128 of its epochs leave 0.69 of the shift, whereas four participants leave about half and 32 leave 0.125" (p. 7, §7.2)

> "with eight participants and eight epochs each, the shift is detected in 97% of draws, yet half of it remains" (p. 7, §7.2)

### Stated conditions and limitations

> "The exact results assume independent calibration, isotropic Gaussian contrasts, a single shift direction, shared linear or affine editing, and the covariance-adjusted residual and distortion." (p. 8, §8 Discussion, Limitations)

> "Like every linear eraser, including LEACE, the editors remove only the first-moment difference." (p. 8, §8, Limitations)

> "Second-moment differences, which can be substantial between devices (Section N), are beyond any linear correction" (p. 8, §8, Limitations)

> "Neither source decodability nor a reduction in source separation alone establishes a downstream benefit" (p. 8, §8, Limitations)

> "Shifts spread over several directions, or more than two sources, call for a subspace version of the frontier." (p. 8, §8, What remains open)

> "Correcting one person means targeting their own shift rather than the population mean" (p. 8, §8, What remains open)

### How it differs from our study

* **Window-selected sub-population.** The residual is due to finite calibration noise in estimating one population direction, assessed on new participants. Our failure is on a selected subset of the fitting cases, where the site difference itself can differ from the full-set difference. They name per-unit shifts as open ("Correcting one person …").
* **Site/centre variable.** Two sources and one shift direction. We have 4 centres; they say more than two sources needs a subspace version.
* **Probe class.** They state that linear erasers remove only first-moment differences, and that second-moment differences are beyond any linear correction. This is consistent with, but does not test, our MLP decodability after LEACE.
* **Learned video representations.** EEG log-power spectra, not video foundation-model embeddings.
* **MTP, SDR estimator.** No causal estimation. They add that source decodability does not by itself establish a downstream effect.

## Q1-c: Impossibility results for fair representations

* **Citation:** arXiv 2107.03483v1 (7 Jul 2021)
* **Access:** Freely available on arXiv under the arXiv non-exclusive distribution licence (not CC; abstract page). Retrieved: arXiv PDF v1, kept locally for verification only and not committed.

### Setting

* **Type of paper:** theory only (definitions, claims, theorems, proofs). No experiments were found in the text.
* **What counts as fair:** a representation is "adversarially fair" when every classifier built on it is group-fair, e.g. demographic parity for two groups.
* **Question:** whether a fixed representation can guarantee this when the marginal distribution of individuals changes between tasks.
* No time dimension and no causal estimation.

> "if every classifier h ∈ HF is group-fair" (p. 6, §3.2, Definition 3 (Adversarial fairness))

> "When the data marginal distribution w.r.t. which the fairness is defined is fixed and available to the designer of a representation, then, as shown by [14] and followup papers, DP fairness is indeed possible." (p. 4, §2 Related Work)

> "While some of our conclusions are intuitive, we formulate (and prove) crisp statements of such impossibilities" (p. 1, Abstract)

### What is shown

* **Size:** for any non-constant classifier, some distribution over the same individuals makes it arbitrarily DP-unfair (the paper's example threshold is unfairness > 0.9). That distribution can be uniform over a subset of the individuals.
* So no non-constant representation stays adversarially DP-fair under every such shift.
* **Our reading (not stated in the paper):** with two groups, if a classifier on the representation is DP-unfair under the shifted distribution, group membership can be predicted above chance from the representation under it. A guarantee that the group cannot be decoded, obtained under one distribution, therefore need not survive restriction to a subset.

> "We show that any fixed representation that allow expressing non-trivial classification cannot guarantee DP fairness in the face of shifting marginal (that is, unlabeled) data distribution (see section 4)." (p. 4, §2 Related Work)

> "For the fairness of these loan allocation decisions, the relevant ratio between the groups is 80/37 rather than the 10,000/20,000 ratio available to the representation designer." (p. 4, §2 Related Work)

> "We show that we cannot guarantee fairness of a fixed data presentation for general shifts of this kind, even for the simplest case of demographic parity." (p. 7, §4 Can there be a generic fair representation?)

> "there exists a probability distribution P over X such that f is arbitrarily DP-unfair w.r.t. P" (p. 7, §4, Claim 1)

> "there will always be a probability distribution over the set of individuals (or, a subset of that set with the uniform distribution over it) relative to which that decision will be acutely Demographic Parity unfair" (p. 7, §4, after Claim 1)

> "No data representation can guarantee the DP fairness of any non-trivial classifier w.r.t. all possible data generating distributions" (p. 7, §4, Corollary 1)

### Stated conditions and limitations

> "We conclude the impossibility of having generic data representations that guarantee (even just) DP fairness with respect to tasks whose marginal distributions are not accessible when designing the representation." (p. 12, §7 Conclusion)

### How it differs from our study

* **Window-selected sub-population.** The bad subset is constructed to exist (worst case); nothing says how likely it is or how large the failure would be for a natural selection rule. Ours is a prespecified clinical window selection, with a measured failure (linear BA 0.39–0.45 vs chance 0.25).
* **Linear erasure, probe class.** General representations and every classifier on them, not LEACE or a specified probe class.
* **Site/centre variable.** Two protected groups, not 4 centres. No time dimension.
* **MTP, SDR estimator, video representations.** No causal estimation and no data.

## Bottom line

* **Q4-a:**
  * The full text confirms the DIRECT classification for generic instruments of time-varying treatments.
  * The amplification it shows is small: about +0.3 percentage points on 4–6 points of baseline bias.
  * It holds for perfect instruments and reverses for near-instruments that are weakly tied to the unmeasured confounder.
  * The setting is two time points, static regimes and parametric g-computation.
  * None of our five dimensions is covered.
* **Q1-a, Q1-b and Q1-c:** the full texts confirm the PARTIAL classification. They show, respectively:
  * LEACE leaking on held-out data under linear probes;
  * the residual left on new participants by finite-calibration linear erasure;
  * that no fixed representation keeps a group-fairness guarantee under every change of the marginal, including restriction to a subset (theory, worst case).
* None of them shows linear erasure failing on a prespecified, selected subset of the fitting sample, and none involves causal estimation.
